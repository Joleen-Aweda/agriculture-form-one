Preparation of brooding house and brooder

This stage involves a brooding period. The brooding period is the time interval from hatching until the chicks no longer require some artificial heat. The brooding period is the most important and difficult stage in the management of chickens. In this stage, chicks are normally reared in the brooder houses that have been placed away from the other stock. It is recommended to select a brooder that is easy to use, cheap, and gets the brooding done well. Good management has also to be adhered to at the arrival of the chicks as well as the day-to-day management of temperature and comfort to chicks.

Preparation before the arrival of chicks:

The brooder should be ready at least 2 weeks before the arrival of chicks. This includes thorough washing and disinfecting the brooder house as well as feed and water troughs and spreading clean, dry, soft and dust-free litter material at a depth of 5 cm. If the number of chicks is not large and it is manageable, litter material is covered with pieces of paper. This helps the chicks to identify food early. The paper cover should have to be changed daily for 2 – 3 days or frequently as they get soiled with droppings. If the number of chicks is large and a relatively large area is required for a brooder, spreading paper might not be practical. In this case, a litter material sieved to reduce dust, spread evenly and compacted to a certain extent would be used. Heat sources should be placed in a well-prepared brooder. Feed and water should be placed in the troughs 12 hours before the arrival of chicks. It is advisable to supplement chicks with proper vitamins and glucose through drinking water to enable them to overcome stresses due to transport and new environment. A veterinarian can be consulted for advice.

Arrival of the chicks:

On arrival, remove the chicks from the box immediately. Separate the dead chicks and those with abnormalities and dispose them. Count and weigh the newly arrived chicks, record each necessary data, and vaccinate them against Marek’s disease if they were not vaccinated in the hatchery. To weigh chicks, pick a few and generalise the weight for the rest. Don’t weigh every single chick. If the chicks’ navels are not dry enough, seek advice from a veterinarian to provide a proper solution. The general characteristics of good chicks are as follow:

  • (i)Dry feathers; not too moist.
  • (ii)Alert and curious with clean, open and bright eyes; eyes should not be dull or closed.
  • (iii)Dry and well-closed navel; not open or with remains of yolk.
  • (iv)Active and move with an upright posture; not with bent legs.
  • (v)Free from any other abnormality, for example, crooked beak and stiff or bent neck.
